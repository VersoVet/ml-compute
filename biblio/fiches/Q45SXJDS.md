An auditable and source-verified framework for clinical AI decision support: integrating retrieval-augmented generation with data provenance.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise in supporting clinical decision making, yet adoption in healthcare remains limited by concerns regarding transparency, verifiability, and accountability of AI-generated recommendations. In particular, generative and data-driven CDS systems often provide outputs without clearly exposing the evidentiary basis or reasoning process underlying their conclusions. This article presents a conceptual framework for auditable and source-verified AI-based clinical decision support, grounded in principles from evidence-based medicine, data provenance, and trustworthy AI. The proposed architecture integrates a curated medical knowledge base with explicit provenance metadata, a retrieval-augmented reasoning (RAG) engine that links generated recommendations to identifiable clinical guidelines and peer-reviewed sources, and a tamper-evident audit logging mechanism that records system inputs, retrieved evidence, and inference steps for retrospective review.

An AI-first healthcare system represents a shift in design philosophy—treating AI as foundational infrastructure for care delivery—rather than a simple accumulation of new isolated technologies. The primary barriers to AI-first healthcare are no longer purely technical but relate to data quality/interoperability, algorithmic bias, explainability, workflow integration, and governance. Human-enabled AI is essential: clinicians should remain accountable for decisions, interpret model outputs within clinical context, and preserve relational aspects of care that AI cannot replicate. Significant evidence gaps persist, including limited prospective multi-site studies, inconsistent operationalization of equity considerations, and underdeveloped governance frameworks for model drift, updating, and accountability. Realizing AI-first healthcare will require coordinated advances in research (system-level outcome evaluations), practice (interoperable infrastructure, human-centered design, workforce training), and policy (safety/transparency standards, post-deployment surveillance, evolved reimbursement models).
